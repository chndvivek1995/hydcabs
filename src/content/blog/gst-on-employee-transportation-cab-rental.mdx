**Verify before publishing.** This article describes the general structure of GST as it applies to employee transportation and cab rental, written in plain language for HR, admin and finance teams. Tax rates, notification numbers, thresholds and the conditions for reverse charge and input tax credit change through CBIC notifications and circulars, and their application depends on the exact nature of the supplier and the recipient. Before this page goes live, and before anyone relies on it for a decision, every statement below must be checked against the current CBIC notifications and confirmed with a qualified tax adviser. Nothing here is tax advice.

Few line items on a corporate transport invoice cause as much back-and-forth between vendors, finance teams and auditors as GST on employee transportation. The confusion is understandable. The same service, a cab with a driver carrying your employees from Kukatpally to HITEC City every working day, can be taxed in different ways depending on how the vendor is constituted, how the vendor has chosen to be taxed, and who is paying the tax to the government. This guide explains the moving parts so that you can read a quote, understand what the GST line means, and ask the right questions before signing.

## How GST on employee transportation is classified

The first question is what the supply is, in GST terms. Employee transportation arranged by an employer is generally treated as renting of a motor vehicle designed to carry passengers, where the cost of fuel is included in the consideration charged to the recipient. This is distinct from passenger transport services sold to individual travellers (a stage carriage or a radio taxi ride), and the distinction matters because the rate structure and the reverse charge rules attach to the category.

Most corporate cab, monthly pick-and-drop and shuttle contracts fall into the renting category because the employer, not the individual employee, is the recipient, and the vehicle plus driver is supplied for a period or a route rather than per passenger ticket. If a contract is structured differently, for example as a pure vehicle lease without a driver, or as a transport service sold per employee ticket, the treatment may differ. Confirm the classification for your specific contract.

## Forward charge: the supplier collects and pays

Under forward charge, the vendor charges GST on the invoice, you pay it to the vendor along with the service fee, and the vendor deposits it with the government. This is the default mechanism for most goods and services, and it is how a GST-registered body corporate transport vendor typically bills a corporate client.

For renting of passenger motor vehicles with fuel, suppliers have commonly had a choice between two rate options under forward charge: a lower concessional rate where the supplier does not claim input tax credit on its own purchases, and a standard rate where the supplier does claim full input tax credit. The commonly cited figures for these two options should be read from the current CBIC rate notification rather than from this page, because they have been amended more than once. What matters for you is that the two options exist, that the vendor must tell you which one it is applying, and that the choice affects both the GST amount on your invoice and your own credit position.

## Reverse charge: the recipient pays the tax

Under the reverse charge mechanism (RCM), the recipient of the service, not the supplier, is liable to pay the GST directly to the government. The supplier issues an invoice without charging GST (or marks it as payable under reverse charge), and the recipient self-assesses and deposits the tax, then claims any credit it is eligible for.

RCM has commonly applied to renting of passenger motor vehicles in a specific situation: when the supplier is not a body corporate (for example a proprietorship or partnership), the supplier is charging the lower concessional rate without taking input tax credit, and the recipient is a body corporate. The logic is that a small non-corporate operator serving a company should not carry the compliance burden; the company does. When the supplier is itself a body corporate, or when it has opted for the standard rate with input tax credit, RCM has generally not applied and forward charge is used instead.

For an HR or finance team this has three practical consequences. First, if your vendor is a non-corporate operator on the concessional rate, your company must be set up to discharge RCM liability and reflect it in its returns. Second, two quotes that look identical before tax can land very differently after tax depending on the vendor's constitution. Third, a vendor's constitution and rate option should be stated in writing in the contract. The exact conditions that trigger RCM are set out in the relevant CBIC notification and must be verified for the current period.

## Input tax credit: what the employer can and cannot claim

Input tax credit (ITC) is the mechanism by which a GST-registered business offsets the tax it has paid on inputs against the tax it collects on its own outputs. Whether an employer can claim ITC on GST paid for employee transport has been one of the most debated questions in this area, and the answer depends on the vehicle, the purpose and the law in force.

The GST law contains a list of blocked credits. Renting or hiring of motor vehicles for carrying passengers has generally been in that list for vehicles with a seating capacity up to a specified number of persons, with exceptions. Two exceptions have commonly been discussed in the context of employee transport. One is where the employer is under a statutory obligation to provide transport to its employees, for example where a state law or a labour regulation requires it for certain shifts or categories of workers. The other relates to vehicles above the seating threshold, such as buses, where the blocking provision has generally not applied. There have also been advance rulings and circulars on specific fact patterns, and they do not all point the same way.

For Hyderabad offices this means a 40-seater bus contract and a sedan contract may have different ITC outcomes even when both carry the same employees, and a night-shift contract for women employees may be treated differently from a general-shift contract if a statutory obligation can be established. None of this should be assumed. The seating threshold, the exact wording of the exceptions and the current position of the authorities must be confirmed against the CBIC notifications and with a qualified adviser before any credit is taken.

## What to check on the invoice

Whichever mechanism applies, the GST section of a transport invoice should make the following clear. Use this table as a checklist when reviewing a quote or a monthly bill.

| Invoice element | Why it matters | What to confirm |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Supplier GSTIN and constitution | Decides whether RCM can apply | Body corporate or not, as per registration |
| Service classification and code | Decides the rate structure | Renting of passenger vehicle with fuel, or other |
| Rate option chosen by supplier | Concessional without ITC or standard with ITC | Stated in the contract and on every invoice |
| Forward charge or reverse charge | Decides who deposits the tax | Marked on the invoice; matches supplier constitution |
| Taxable value breakdown | Tolls, parking and escort may be treated differently | Which items carry GST and which are pass-through |
| Place of supply | Decides CGST plus SGST or IGST | Hyderabad to Hyderabad is normally intra-state |
| Your ITC eligibility | Decides your net cost | Vehicle seating, statutory obligation, current law |

Tolls and parking deserve a separate note. Some vendors bill them as reimbursements at actuals, others fold them into the taxable value. The correct treatment depends on how the contract is written and on the GST valuation rules, and it changes the GST amount on the invoice. Agree the treatment in writing.

## Common situations for Hyderabad offices

A registered company in Gachibowli hires a body corporate vendor for [monthly pick and drop](/services/monthly-cab-for-office-hyderabad/). Forward charge applies; the vendor shows GST on the invoice at the rate option it has chosen. The company's ITC position depends on vehicle seating and the blocked-credit rules.

A registered company in Pocharam hires a proprietorship operator on the concessional rate for sedans. RCM has commonly applied here: the operator invoices without GST, the company self-assesses and pays. The company must have RCM processes in place and must examine its ITC eligibility on the tax it pays under RCM.

A manufacturing unit in Patancheru contracts a bus operator for shift transport through an [employee shuttle and bus service](/services/employee-shuttle-bus-hyderabad/). Because the vehicle is above the usual seating threshold, the ITC blocking provision has generally not applied, but the forward or reverse charge question still turns on the operator's constitution and rate option.

A company whose women employees work night shifts and which is required by applicable state rules to provide transport for them may be able to argue statutory obligation for ITC on those legs. Whether this is available, and for which legs, is a question for your adviser with the current rules in hand. ## Checklist before signing a transport contract

- Record the vendor's GSTIN and confirm whether it is a body corporate
- Record the rate option the vendor is applying and the notification it relies on
- Confirm whether invoices will be under forward charge or reverse charge, and set up RCM processes if required
- Agree in writing how tolls, parking and escort charges are treated for GST
- Confirm the vehicle classes and seating capacities, since they affect ITC
- Ask your tax adviser to document your ITC position for each contract type
- Diarise an annual review, because CBIC notifications change
