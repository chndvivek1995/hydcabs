If you have ever compared the kilometres on your transport invoice with the distance between your employees' homes and the office, you have probably noticed a gap. That gap is made up of dead kilometres in employee transport: distance a vehicle covers with nobody on board. Dead km are not fraud and they are not a vendor trick. They are an unavoidable part of running vehicles from a hub to scattered pickups and back. But their size is very much within your control, and in a city shaped like Hyderabad, with offices concentrated in the western corridor and homes spread from Kompally to LB Nagar, they can quietly become one of the largest avoidable items on the bill.

This article explains where dead kilometres come from, why they are hard to see, and eight practical ways to cut them without compromising pickup times or safety rules.

## What counts as dead kilometres in employee transport

A dead kilometre (also called an empty km, deadhead km or non-revenue km) is any distance a vehicle travels during your contracted service without an employee in it. The live kilometres are the ones from the first pickup to the office, or from the office to the last drop. Everything else is dead.

In a per-employee pricing model, dead km are built into the per-seat rate; you never see them but you pay for them. In a dedicated-vehicle model with a monthly km cap, dead km eat into the cap and push you towards extra km charges. In either case the arithmetic is simple: total km multiplied by the rate per km is the bill, and dead km are the part of total km that moves nobody.

## The five sources of dead kilometres

### 1. Hub to first pickup

The vendor's vehicles start the day somewhere, usually a hub or the driver's home. If the hub is in Jeedimetla and the first pickup is in Manikonda, the vehicle drives across the city empty before it earns anything. This is the largest single source for most contracts, and it is decided entirely by where vehicles are based relative to where your employees live.

### 2. Last drop to hub

The mirror image. After the morning run ends at your office in Gachibowli, the vehicle returns to its base, or waits near the office. After the evening run ends at the last employee's home in Uppal, the vehicle drives back empty. For night shifts this is especially heavy: a 2 AM last drop in Secunderabad followed by an empty run back to a western hub is a long dead leg at a time when no other trip is available to fill it.

### 3. Repositioning between runs

A vehicle that finishes a 9 AM login run at HITEC City and must be at Kukatpally for a 2 PM shift pickup drives empty between the two. Rotational shift patterns with staggered logins create more of these gaps than a single-shift office does.

### 4. Inefficient pickup sequencing

Even on a live run, bad sequencing adds distance. A sedan that picks up an employee in Kondapur, then goes back out to Miyapur for the second pickup, then returns through Kondapur to reach Madhapur has covered a chunk of the route twice. Strictly speaking those are live km (someone is on board), but they are wasted km that behave like dead km on the bill.

### 5. No-shows and late cancellations

A vehicle that drives to a pickup point for an employee who does not turn up has made a detour for nothing. Repeated no-shows on the same route, often from employees who switched to working from home without updating the roster, can add a surprising amount of unproductive distance over a month.

## Why dead kilometres hide in your bill

Most invoices show either a per-employee charge by slab or a per-vehicle charge with total km. Neither line separates live and dead distance. The vendor knows the split, because their GPS data records every leg, but unless you ask for it you will not see it. The result is that two quotes with the same per-km rate can produce very different monthly totals, simply because one vendor positions vehicles closer to your pickup belt than the other. ## Hyderabad examples

Consider an office near the Gachibowli flyover with day-shift employees living mainly in Kukatpally, Miyapur and Chandanagar. If the vendor's vehicles are based around Kukatpally or Miyapur, the hub-to-first-pickup leg is short and the vehicles arrive at the office full. After the drop they can either wait near [Gachibowli](/locations/gachibowli/) for the evening run or take another client's reverse trip. Dead km are a small share of total km.

Now move the same office to [Kokapet](/locations/kokapet/) and the same employees to Uppal and LB Nagar. The sensible live route uses the ORR, which is fast but long, and the vehicles must either stay in Kokapet all day or cross the city twice. Dead km as a share of total km climb sharply, and so does the per-employee cost, even though nothing about the vendor's rate card has changed.

Night shifts magnify the effect again. A Pocharam contact centre's 1 AM drops fan out to Uppal, Secunderabad and Kukatpally; each vehicle then returns empty with no reverse load available until the 6 AM login run. This is a structural feature of any [night shift cab service](/services/night-shift-cab-hyderabad/), and it is why night routing is designed around vehicle positioning as much as around pickup sequence.

## Eight ways to cut dead kilometres

- Ask where vehicles will be based. This is the single most effective question in any vendor evaluation. Vehicles parked near your pickup belt, not near the office, cut the hub-to-first-pickup leg for the morning run.
- Pair your shifts with another client's reverse flow. A vendor serving several companies can use a vehicle that drops your Kukatpally staff at HITEC City at 9 AM to carry another client's HITEC City residents to Kukatpally. This is the main structural advantage of a multi-client vendor over an in-house fleet.
- Stagger logins within a window, not across the day. Four logins between 8:30 and 9:30 AM let one vehicle run two short loops; logins at 8 AM, 11 AM, 2 PM and 5 PM force empty repositioning between each.
- Clean the roster weekly. Remove employees on leave, on hybrid days and those who have left. Every removed no-show is a removed detour.
- Use automated routing, not driver judgement, for pickup sequence. Routing software sequences stops to minimise total distance and respects first-pickup and last-drop rules for women employees at night; a driver working from a messaging-app list cannot do both reliably. See our [transport management solution](/services/transport-management-solution/).
- Cluster pickups at junctions where safe and acceptable. A single stop at Kukatpally Y Junction or Miyapur Metro for three employees who live within a few hundred metres removes three doorstep detours. Keep doorstep pickup for night legs and for anyone who needs it.
- Convert dense corridors to shuttle loops. A tempo traveller on a fixed Miyapur to Gachibowli loop has almost no dead km because it turns straight around. The [employee shuttle service](/services/employee-shuttle-bus-hyderabad/) page explains when a corridor is dense enough.
- Review the live-to-dead ratio monthly and rebalance. Vehicle bases, routes and rosters all drift. A standing monthly review keeps dead km from creeping back.

## How to measure them: the report to ask for

Ask your vendor for a monthly route-level report with the following columns. If they cannot produce it, their GPS data is not being used for your benefit.

| Metric | What it tells you | What good looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Live km per route | Distance with employees on board | Stable month to month |
| Dead km per route | Empty legs: hub, return, repositioning | Falling or steady as a share of total |
| Live-to-total km ratio | Share of distance that is productive | Higher is better; compare routes against each other |
| Average occupancy per trip | Seats filled against seats available | High on day routes; lower is expected at night |
| No-shows per route | Pickups attempted with no boarding | Near zero after roster cleaning |
| Repositioning km | Empty travel between consecutive runs | Low when logins are staggered within a window |
| Vehicle base location | Where each vehicle starts and ends | Near the pickup belt, not the office |

Track these for three months and you will see exactly which routes, shifts and vehicle bases are generating empty distance. Most of the fixes above need no contract change; they need a roster, a map and a vendor willing to show you the data.
